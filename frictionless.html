McGraw Hill Ed­u­ca­tion

Teachers couldn't find content that was already in the platform.

McGraw Hill's Open Learning Platform held K-12 instructional content for teachers and students, but its structure made it hard to navigate. Teachers drilled through layer after layer to find a single resource, in the middle of class, when speed mattered most.

Focus
  • Navigation
  • Information architecture
Scope
  • Teachers
  • Administrators
  • Reviewers
Context
  • Edtech
  • State-level adoptions

The hierarchy made simple tasks feel harder than they should have.

The platform's content model was deeply nested, with multiple layers that could each hold content or simply act as containers. In practice, teachers often had to click through several levels before reaching anything usable.

That slowed teachers down and broke their flow. For administrators reviewing the platform at a state level, it also made it harder to see what was actually in the curriculum.

Teachers and reviewers needed different things from the same structure.

The problem wasn't the same for every user. Teachers often knew exactly what they were trying to find and needed to get there fast. Reviewers were looking for the opposite: a high-level view that made the breadth and structure of the curriculum easier to understand.

The redesign had to work for both at once.

The existing navigation asked for too much effort up front.

To reach the course structure, teachers first had to open a separate Browse This Course view, then decide whether to enter a container or expand it, then continue drilling down until something actionable appeared.

That was slow, especially during a live lesson. The deeper the structure went, the more work it took just to find where the useful content lived.

Too many clicks were spent uncovering structure instead of accessing content.

The hierarchy was complex, and it was also hidden. Teachers had to open it up one level at a time before they could decide where to go next.

That made even well-organized content feel harder to use than it actually was.

The redesign made the course structure visible.

The redesign kept the course hierarchy in view the whole time. Teachers stopped going in and out of containers and moved through the course directly.

A navigation panel on the left stayed open, so teachers could reach any content without losing their place. A full-page table of contents showed the whole course at once.

The redesign kept the complexity and made it navigable.

The content still needed to support a wide range of educational structures, products, and roles. The work left that system intact and made it easier for people to see where they were and what was available.

The platform got easier to use in a real classroom, and easier to read for people evaluating it from outside.

Teachers reached lesson content without drilling through containers.

With the updated navigation in place, teachers no longer had to drill through intermediate containers to reach instructional materials. Teachers could see the content hierarchy at a glance, and the table of contents was reachable from every content page.

The full-screen table of contents also gave administrators and reviewers the whole curriculum in context, so they understood its structure and scope faster. That mattered in state evaluations, where how easily reviewers found things affected whether the curriculum got adopted.

Better navigation helped most during live instruction.

The persistent panel and full-screen table of contents cut out the clicking it used to take to reach a lesson.

Teachers kept their flow, even when they switched to new content mid-lesson.

Fewer clicks to a lesson

Teachers reached lesson materials without drilling through containers.

Navigation stays in view

The panel stays open beside the lesson, so teachers move between topics without losing their place.

Built for live instruction

Teachers could switch to new content mid-lesson with very little interruption.

The table of contents on every page

Wherever a teacher was in the course, Browse This Course opened the table of contents.

The friction here came from a structure people couldn't see.

The content didn't change. Teachers could finally see where it was, and reviewers could see all of it at once.
